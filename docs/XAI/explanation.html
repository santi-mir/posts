Thoughts on AI

Explanation

This post is an introduction to explanations.

What is an explanation?

A concise definition of an explanation was given in Studies in The Logic of Explanation (Hempel and Oppenheim, 1948):

To explain the phenomena in the world of our experience, to answer the question "why?" rather than only the question "what?"; is one of the foremost objectives of rational enquiry (...)

The paper How People Explain Action (and Autonomous Intelligent Systems Should Too) (Malle et. al., 2017) highlights a structural aspect of explanations:

Explanation is arguably a three-value predicate: someone, a communicator, explains something to someone, an audience. The success of an explanation therefore depends on several critical audience factors—assumptions, knowledge, and interests that an audience has when decoding the explanation.

Explaining Explanations in AI (Wachter et. al., 2019) also defines of explanations with emphasis on complex models:

(...) "explanation" refers to numerous ways of exchanging information about a phenomenon, in this case the functionality of a model or the rationale and criteria for a decision, to different stakeholders (Lipton, 2016; Miller, 2017).

In the book "Why we ask why?" (2026), Tania Lombrozo defines explanation in similar terms to Hempel and Oppenheim's:

Explanations are responses to actual or implied why- or how-questions that offer understanding about what's being explained.

Explanation in artificial intelligence: insights from the social sciences (Miller, 2019; citing Lombrozo) states that explanation is both a process and a product (the result).

These ideas can be combined as below:

  • Someone explains something (the explanandum) to someone, in two steps:
    1. Cognitive Process: explanatory hypotheses are generated (aiming for understanding),
    2. Social Process: the hypotheses are communicated to an audience and / or ourselves.

All or some of the steps may repeat; for example, by refining the explanandum and re-evaluating the hypotheses. Eventually, one hypothesis, the "best explanation", is selected until it is either:

  • Contradicted by experience (falsified),
  • Superseded by a simpler or more general one,
  • Shown to be inconsistent with prior knowledge,
  • Other reasons (list is not exhaustive).

Note

This definitions are just a starting point, and only cover a subset of instances of what we'd call explanations.

Scientific Explanation

Scientific explanation is a type of explanation, so it inherits all the characteristics mentioned earlier.

What makes it scientific is a complex topic. On the whole, scientific explanations tend to be general and supported by large bodies of knowledge; the hypotheses need to be falsifiable, and the selection process involves rigorous proofs or experimental testing.

The paper "From scientific theory to duality of predictive artificial intelligence models" describes what constitutes scientific understanding of a phenomenon (references removed) as:

(...) if there is an underlying intelligible theory such that scientists can qualitatively comprehend the consequences of this theory without the need for quantitative analysis and exact calculations.

Now that the problem has drifted from defining explanation to refining what the cognitive and social process should look like, let's dive into them.

The Cognitive Process

The cognitive process involves understanding the explanandum and generating possible hypotheses that led to it.

Contrastive Explanations

Explanations are contrastive, as Miller et al., put it:

Perhaps the most important result from this work is that explanations are contrastive; or more accurately, why–questions are contrastive. That is, why–questions are of the form "Why P rather than Q?", where P is the fact that requires explanation, and Q is some foil case that was expected.

Even "Why P?" questions usually have an implicit foil. Foils can make answering questions easier, by only focusing on the differences between the two cases.

The paper Beware of Inmates Running the Asylum has an example using implicit foils:

(...) "Why did Mr. Jones open the window?" with the response "Because he was hot" is not useful if the implied foil is Mr. Jones turning on the air conditioner, as this explains both the fact and the foil; or if the implied foil was why Ms. Smith, who was sitting closer to the window, did not open it instead, as the cited cause does not refer to a cause of Ms. Smith's lack of action.

Let's now dive deeper into the problem of selecting useful foils.

Selecting Foils

When the number of variables is large simulating all the combinations is infeasible, so humans must select a few counterfactuals to simulate, but how?

Miller et. al. consider this problem:

However, simulating an entire causal chain is infeasible in most cases, so cognitive scientists and social psychologists have studied how people decide which events to 'undo' (the counterfactuals) to determine cause. For example, people tend to undo more proximal causes over more distal causes [Miller and Gunasegaram, 1990], abnormal events over normal events [Kahneman and Tversky, 1982], and events that are considered more 'controllable' [Girotto et al., 1991].

Hesslow considers a similar set: proximal over distal events (in the causal chain of events); abnormal or unexpected events; controllable events, deviation from theoretical ideals, model, predictive power, responsibility, and so forth. He concludes that most of those are taking care of by contrastive why-questions which compare the event to be explained to a reference case (particular instance or general case). He states (bold is mine):

Many of the selection criteria listed in Section 3 can be construed as the result of choosing different objects of comparison or reference classes. Let us consider again the fire in the barn, and let us suppose that we have in the back of our minds the picture of a normal barn. (...) the normal barn has not caught fire, it follows that an explanatorily relevant condition for this barn's catching fire must be abnormal. Thus, selection of abnormal conditions can be viewed as the result of comparing the explanandum object with a normal object.

And also most other causal selections are contained:

(...) the difference between this barn now and this barn yesterday, i.e. we would be selecting a precipitating cause [proximal in the list above]. Selection of the unexpected may be viewed as the result of explaining the difference between an expected and an actual outcome. Selection according to responsibility follows from a comparison between actual and morally ideal behaviour. Selection of conditions which cause a deviation from a theoretical ideal involves a comparison between an actual and a theoretically ideal situation, and so on (cf. Hesslow, 1983).

Then Hesslow writes the strategy in a short paragraph:

What I want to suggest, then, is that the explanandum should be construed as a relation which involves three things: an object a, an object of comparison b and an explanandum property E which a has and b does not have.

Here is yet another illustration by Hesslow, of how contrasts cases narrow down possible causes:

For instance, if we want to explain why the fly Ml has shorter wings than Nl, then the temperature in which the flies were raised is explanatorily irrelevant, since the temperature was the same in both cases. The mutated gene on the other hand was present in one case and absent in the other. It is, therefore, explanatorily relevant.

However, selecting a useful reference case (or foil) isn't trivial. As Explaining Explanations in AI states:

However, choosing a relevant set of cases or events against which contrastive explanations are provided is not a straightforward challenge. (...)

The Idea of a Counterfactual

Assigning causes is Miller et. al. "necessary to provide explanations", and foils can help us focus in the relevant causes1.

To the extent our question is answered by identifying and inferring a cause, the process of explaining requires causal inference.

Counterfactuals are one way to define causes. It means contrary to the facts; that is, a hypothetical or imaginary world. But counterfactuals are not restricted to their role defining causes; they relate to causes because they can be used to define causes.

Note

Defining Cause via Counterfactuals Assume that an event B happened. Then, if B couldn't have happen without A, we can say that A is a cause of B or that "B wouldn't have happened had A not happened".

Notice that this definition isn't a necessarily a physical cause. It is an epistemological framing of causes (we are defining it as such). This becomes relevant in the case of models of phenomena as well.

Going back to our task at hand "Why P rather than Q?" (or "Why P?" with an implicit foil (maybe "rather than not P")) involves finding a cause.

Either way, the cause "X" can't lead to both P and Q! If so, it can't explain why only one of them happened. So we want a case where "X" makes "P" likely but not "Q".

In this case we are using the counterfactual: Had X not happened, P wouldn't have happened and Q could have happened. The contrast will help us reduce the number of causes to consider.

So far the idea is that we are answering "Why P rather than Q?" and this involves assigning a cause, which is an event (or something) that leads to P but not to Q.

Next, we will continue to explore contrastive explanations but change the question slightly.

"Shifting" Counterfactual Contrastive Explanations

Instead of the question "Why P rather than Q?" we can ask "What minimal changes (to the inputs) take us from P to Q (or P')?". This close to what Counterfactual explanations without opening the black box (...) asks:

We define Counterfactual Explanations as statements taking the form: Score p was returned because variables V had values (v1, v2,...) associated with them. If V instead had values (v1', v2',...), and all other variables had remained constant, score p' would have been returned.

While many such explanations are possible, an ideal counterfactual explanation would alter values as little as possible and represent a closest world under which score p' is returned instead of p. The notion of a "closest possible world" is thus implicit in our definition.

A more descriptive name would be something like "Shifting Counterfactual-Contrastive Explanations" since we are "shifting" the input state with the goal of "shifting" the output state (to the contrast).

Another kind of counterfactual seeks for the output to remain the same rather than to change (for fairness purposes); this could be called "Shifting-Counterfactual Contrastive Explanation". They state:

Kusner et al.58 consider counterfactuals where the subject belongs to a different race or sex, and require that the decision made remain the same under such a counterfactual for it to be considered fair. In contrast, we consider counterfactuals in which the decision differs from its current state.

Often, multiple counterfactual explanations may be preferred, so that the subject can choose the optimal one.

Causal Explanations in AI

Machine Learning Models are often doing correlation, so the assignment of causes may lead us astray, since it does not correspond to a real structure in the world (for that case, it should be based on a causal graph).

Building models where causal variables and causal graphs are preferred is of interest for certain modelling problems.

Explaining Explanations in AI is clear about it:

In any case, our approach does not rely on knowledge of the causal structure of the world, (...)

Pearl has a "Ladder of Causation" for causal attribution (assigning causes):

  1. Association / correlation: can't assign causes,
  2. Interventions: experiments where X is a cause if P(Y∣do(Xi​))>P(Y) (other variables remain constant) i.e. X increases the probability of the outcome; also observations in double blind, randomised controlled trials (RCTs),
  3. Cause-finding Counterfactuals: require that we have a causal graph.

Social Process (Communication)

We have gone through the cognitive process and how contrastive questions can aid the generation and selection of a hypothesis or a cause.

The second process is that of communication. This isn't just the transfer of knowledge; in the best cases, it involves an exchange between the participants (explainer and explainee), as in a e.g. dialogue.

The communication can be aided by the gricean maxims: rules of effective communication.

  • Informative (Quantity): right amount of context and details,
  • Truthful (Quality, or Fidelity): the explanation should be true,
  • Relevance (Relation): avoid presumed-known or superfluous details, focus on what provides insight,
    • One example given earlier is to focus on unexpected events, whilst ignoring what is presumed to be known by the listener.
  • Manner (clarity): express it in elegant terms.

In some cases, humans also tend to prefer concrete over abstract explanations, so "concreteness" could be added to the list.

Relevance is primarily related to the causal selection problem in relation to an audience, as Malle et. al., state:

How do people solve this problem? They determine what exact question the audience is interested in (McClure and Hilton 1998); they take into account what their audience member already knows (Slugoski et al. 1993); and they offer elements of explanations that build bridges between presumed knowledge and novel information (Korman and Malle 2016). In short, they offer explanations that generate coherence in a knowledge structure of old and new information (Thagard 1989).

Contrastive explanations can also take care of many of these aspects automatically, by selecting a contrast that is relevant or understood by the audience.
